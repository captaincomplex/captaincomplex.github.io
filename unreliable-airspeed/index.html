Unreliable airspeed

Four steps through the procedure, from the first odd number on the PFD to the landing. Each step has an instrument you can fly or break.

Step 1 of 4

Recognise it

Unreliable speed rarely arrives with a clean warning. The first job is to notice that the indications no longer match what the aircraft is doing with the pitch and thrust you have set.

Typical cues

  • Speed disagreement between the captain PFD, the F/O PFD and the standby instrument.
  • Speed that fluctuates, freezes, or changes in a way that does not match pitch and thrust.
  • Indicated speed rising in a climb and falling in a descent, like an altimeter.
  • Altitude disagreement, or altitude and vertical speed frozen.
  • STALL or OVERSPEED warnings that contradict at least one speed indication.
  • Odd AP, FD or A/THR behaviour, or an unexpected AP disconnection.
  • Aerodynamic noise that does not fit the indicated speed.
  • ECAM alerts such as NAV ADR DISAGREE, NAV IAS DISCREPANCY or NAV ALTI DISCREPANCY. Not always: if two ADRs are wrong in the same way, the system can reject the good one.

Common causes

  • Pitot probes blocked by ice: ice crystals at high altitude, or severe icing.
  • Heavy rain overwhelming the pitot drain holes.
  • Insects or nests in the probes after time on the ground.
  • Pitot covers or static-port tape left on after maintenance or washing.
  • Static ports blocked or damaged, for example by a bird strike or a radome problem.

Speed comes from the difference between total pressure (pitot) and static pressure. Altitude comes from static pressure alone. Block one side and you can predict which numbers go wrong, and in which direction.

What the items are for

AP, FD and A/THR are all fed by the air data that has just gone wrong. Turning them off stops them chasing a false speed. Both FDs go off, so no bars are left commanding the wrong pitch.

The pitch and thrust pairs keep the aircraft safely away from both stall and overspeed across normal weights, without any speed indication at all. THR RED ALT is the thrust reduction altitude from the takeoff.

Hold them only for a short time. Once at or above the MSA or circuit altitude, level off and troubleshoot (step 3).

The configuration lines (flaps, speedbrakes, gear) and their exact wording differ between FCOM revisions and operators. Some standards treat them as memory items, others as read-and-do. Learn the version in your own QRH.

Step 3 of 4

Stabilise, then troubleshoot

With the aircraft level and on table values, you have time. Now find out which air data you can trust, and get rid of the rest.

QRHThe sequence

  • Level off at or above the MSA or circuit altitude. Set the pitch and N1 from the QRH level-flight table for your weight and altitude, keep the configuration.
  • Cross-check the three ADRs. ADR 1 drives the captain PFD, ADR 2 the F/O PFD. Use the AIR DATA selector to put ADR 3 on one side and compare it.
  • Compare each one with the table speed for the pitch and N1 you are flying, with GPS ground speed, and with the stall warning.
  • Switch off the faulty ADR(s) on the overhead ADIRS panel, and use the AIR DATA selector to give a PFD back its speed from ADR 3.
  • If no ADR can be trusted, fly the BUSS where fitted, or the pitch and thrust tables.

What stays trustworthy

  • GPS ground speed on the ND. Compare it with the TAS you expect plus or minus a known wind. Do not use the ND wind: it is computed from ADR true airspeed, so it is wrong too.
  • Attitude and thrust. The IRs and engine indications do not depend on the pitot-static system.
  • Angle of attack. The stall warning is AoA-based and should be respected. VLS, VαPROT, VMAX and the overspeed warning are computed from the faulty speed.
  • Majority is not proof. Two ADRs agreeing with each other can both be wrong when the same icing hits both probes.

Switching off two or more ADRs puts the flight controls into alternate law and removes the protections. AP and A/THR may not be available afterwards.

BUSS: speed from angle of attack

On aircraft fitted with the back-up speed scale, switching all three ADRs off replaces the PFD speed tape with the BUSS. It is driven by angle of attack, not pressure, and shows bands instead of knots: keep the yellow reference line in the green. GPS altitude replaces barometric altitude.

Without BUSS, the QRH has you keep one ADR on when none can be identified as reliable, and fly the pitch and thrust tables. Keeping one ADR keeps the stall warning available.

Band shapes and colours are simplified here. Whether BUSS is fitted, and the exact switching steps, depend on the aircraft standard.

Step 4 of 4

Approach and landing

Plan a long, unhurried approach. Each configuration change in level flight, each one followed by the table's pitch and N1, then a stabilised descent you check against ground speed.

Configuration and pitch / thrust

  • Use the QRH approach table: for each configuration it gives pitch and N1 for level flight and for a 3° descent, by weight.
  • Extend flaps and gear in level flight, one stage at a time. Set the new pitch and N1 and let it settle before the next stage.
  • Be fully configured early, then start down on the glide path. Use ILS where available, and the FPV (bird) at −3°. Rate of descent for 3° is about 5 × ground speed.
  • In alternate law, extending the gear gives direct law: trim manually (USE MAN PITCH TRIM on the PFD).

Speed cross-checks

  • Compare GPS ground speed with what you expect: VAPP converted to true airspeed, minus the headwind. Use the tower or ATIS wind, not the ND wind.
  • The stall warning stays a valid warning. VLS, VαPROT and the overspeed warning on the PFD come from bad air data.
  • Landing distance increases. Apply the QRH factor or margin for unreliable speed and check the runway is long enough before you commit.
  • Go-around: TOGA and about 15° pitch, then back to the tables.
