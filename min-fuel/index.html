Minimum Fuel

Two radio calls, MINIMUM FUEL and MAYDAY FUEL, both protect one number: the final reserve fuel planned on your flight plan. Neither is about what the gauges read now. Both are about what you predict you will land with. Here's where that fuel comes from, when each call is due, and a holding scenario you can run until the calls make sense.

Two calls, one number

MINIMUM FUEL

When
You are committed to land at a specific aerodrome, and any change to your existing clearance to it may result in landing with less than the planned final reserve fuel.
Means
All your planned aerodrome options are down to one. Not an emergency yet, but one is possible if there is any more delay.
Priority
None. ATC acknowledges, tells you of any expected delay, and passes the information on when it hands you over.
ATC says
ROGER, with any delay information (for example NO DELAY EXPECTED).

MAYDAY, MAYDAY, MAYDAY, FUEL

When
The usable fuel you calculate you will have on landing at the nearest aerodrome where a safe landing can be made is less than the planned final reserve fuel.
Means
A distress call. Final reserve is no longer protected.
Priority
Yes, as for any emergency.
Then
Land at the nearest suitable aerodrome. Final reserve is a reserve, not a floor: it is there to be used to land safely.

The wording above follows ICAO Annex 6 Part I (4.3.7.2), EU Air Ops CAT.OP.MPA.185 as amended by Regulation (EU) 2021/1296 (applied from 30 October 2022), and the UK version of the same rule (Aviation Safety (Amendment) Regulations 2024, in force from 30 October 2025). The UK CAA's guidance adds that "the pilot should not expect any form of priority handling" after a MINIMUM FUEL call.

Two things people get wrong

Myth

“Calling MINIMUM FUEL gets me priority.”

What the rules say

It doesn't. MINIMUM FUEL is information: it tells ATC that your options have narrowed to one aerodrome and that more delay could turn this into an emergency. ATC acknowledges and keeps you informed of delays. If you need priority, the call is MAYDAY FUEL (or PAN, if that fits the situation better).

Myth

“I'll call when the gauges get down to final reserve.”

What the rules say

Both calls compare a prediction with a planned figure. The prediction is the fuel you calculate you will land with. The planned figure is the final reserve on your flight plan, worked out before departure. With 1,500 kg on the gauges and 15 minutes to run at 40 kg a minute, you land with about 900 kg. If the planned final reserve is 1,100 kg, MAYDAY FUEL is due now, while the gauges still read well above it.

Where the fuel comes from

Under the EU and UK rules, the usable fuel you start with must cover a list of components. The flight plan works them out; your job in flight is to keep checking that what's left still covers what's ahead. Here is an illustrative A320 flight plan.

Taxi200 kg
Not less than what you expect to use before take-off, with local conditions and APU use.
Trip5,000 kg
Take-off (or the in-flight replanning point) to landing at destination.
Contingency250 kg
For the unforeseen. In the basic scheme, 5% of trip fuel, and never less than 5 minutes' holding at 1,500 ft above the destination. Approved variations allow 3% with an en-route alternate, 20 minutes' flying time, or a statistical figure.
Destination alternate1,200 kg
Destination to alternate, including the missed approach. With no alternate required, at least 15 minutes' holding at 1,500 ft above the destination instead.
Final reserve1,100 kg
30 minutes at holding speed at 1,500 ft above the aerodrome, in standard conditions, at the expected landing mass (45 minutes for piston aeroplanes). This is the planned figure both calls protect.
Additional0 kg here
Only when the operation needs it: for example an engine or pressurisation failure at the critical point, or reaching an en-route alternate, when the fuel above isn't enough.

For anticipated delays or specific operational constraints, added at planning.

Whatever more the commander decides to carry.

The kilograms are illustrative, for a short A320 sector. Final reserve depends on landing mass and on the type: an A320neo burns less in the hold than an A320ceo. Your flight plan gives the real figures. Component names and amounts follow CAT.OP.MPA.181 and its AMC; your operator's fuel scheme may use approved variations.

This simulation needs JavaScript. The rule it shows: diversion is possible while the fuel you would land with at the alternate is at least the planned final reserve. Once it isn't, you are committed to the destination and MINIMUM FUEL is due. If the fuel you would land with at the destination then falls below the planned final reserve, MAYDAY FUEL is due.

Your fuel figures come from your flight plan and FMS, and your operator's procedures say how to apply the calls.

Is it MINIMUM FUEL, MAYDAY FUEL, or neither?

  1. Situation 1

    You're holding at destination, EAT in 15 minutes. If you divert now, you land at the alternate with final reserve plus 400 kg. If you wait, you land at destination with final reserve plus 700 kg.

    Answer

    Neither. You still have two options and both protect final reserve. Keep the delay information coming from a reliable source and keep checking the latest time you can leave for the alternate.

  2. Situation 2

    Ten minutes later, still in the hold. Diverting now would land you at the alternate below final reserve. Waiting for the EAT still lands you at destination with final reserve plus 300 kg.

    Answer

    MINIMUM FUEL. The alternate has gone; you are committed to the destination, and any change to your clearance may put you below planned final reserve. This is the UK CAA's own example: declare it once your fuel no longer allows a diversion from the hold.

  3. Situation 3

    You've declared MINIMUM FUEL. ATC: runway change, expect 12 more minutes. You now predict landing at destination, the nearest aerodrome where you can land safely, with 150 kg less than the planned final reserve.

    Answer

    MAYDAY FUEL. The calculated landing fuel at the nearest suitable aerodrome is below planned final reserve. That is the trigger, even though you are still airborne with fuel above that figure.

  4. Situation 4

    The runway is blocked; you go around and divert. ATC clears you direct to the alternate. You calculate landing there with final reserve plus 100 kg.

    Answer

    MINIMUM FUEL. You are committed to the alternate and close to final reserve, so any change to your clearance may put you below it. The UK CAA's guidance uses this case as an example.

  5. Situation 5

    In the cruise, headwinds are stronger than forecast. The FMS still predicts landing at destination with more than alternate fuel plus final reserve.

    Answer

    Neither. You've used some contingency fuel, which is what it's for. Keep checking actual against planned burn.

  6. Situation 6

    The gauges read 1,500 kg. The planned final reserve is 1,100 kg. The nearest suitable aerodrome is 15 minutes away and you're burning about 40 kg a minute.

    Answer

    MAYDAY FUEL. You'll land with about 900 kg, below the planned 1,100. The gauges reading above final reserve doesn't matter: the calls are about predicted landing fuel.

  7. Situation 7

    You declared MINIMUM FUEL a few minutes ago. ATC: “Roger, expect 10 minutes delay.” After that delay you still predict landing with final reserve plus 500 kg, and you're committed to this aerodrome.

    Answer

    Neither: no new call. MINIMUM FUEL stands, and it doesn't buy priority, so the delay is normal. Keep recalculating; if the prediction drops below planned final reserve, call MAYDAY FUEL.

  8. Situation 8

    You're committed to a destination with one runway, no alternate within reach, and you predict landing with final reserve plus 50 kg. ATC says no delay expected.

    Answer

    MINIMUM FUEL. Committed to one aerodrome, and any change to the clearance, even a short one, may put you below planned final reserve. “No delay expected” today doesn't change that.

Managing fuel in flight

Keep checking. The rules require continual reanalysis of the planning assumptions in flight: actual burn against plan, and the fuel you will land with at destination and alternate. On the A320 the FMS predicts fuel on board at each waypoint and at destination; most FMS standards also warn when the destination prediction drops below the minimum you set, normally alternate plus final reserve. Your FCOM has the details for your standard.

Get delay information from a reliable source. When something unforeseen means you might land with less than final reserve plus what you need to reach an alternate, you must get delay information from a reliable source; ICAO's wording is to request it from ATC. The UK CAA's guidance asks for delay data you can trust, ideally with a margin (“delay 15 ± 2 minutes”).

Decide where you're going. Committing to a single aerodrome, and so giving up the alternate, is a decision to make with good information: reliable delay figures, weather, runway options. Once you've committed and any change could put you below planned final reserve, declare MINIMUM FUEL.

If final reserve can't be protected, declare and land. MAYDAY FUEL is due as soon as the prediction at the nearest suitable aerodrome is below planned final reserve. Then use whatever landing option is safest; final reserve fuel is usable fuel.

In-flight replanning. If you replan to a different destination or route, the fuel from that point must again cover trip, contingency, alternate, final reserve, and any additional and extra fuel.

Record it. Operators must record fuel data, including any MINIMUM FUEL or MAYDAY FUEL declarations.

Outside ICAO wording: in the United States, the FAA's Aeronautical Information Manual uses “minimum fuel” as an advisory that you can accept little or no delay, and expects an emergency to be declared explicitly if fuel becomes critical. The idea is similar; the details and the reserves are different.
